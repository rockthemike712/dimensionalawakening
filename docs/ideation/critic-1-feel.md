## THE CRITIQUES

**A1 — The Short Way Around (25).** The pinch gesture is a genuine toy and it is the fold verb with one more degree of freedom, which is the correct instinct. But the payoff is *nine seconds of holding a d-pad in a straight line* to bump into your own footprints — that is homework, not a moment. And the author's own risk note is the verdict: on this near-orthographic top-down camera a deficit-angle cone reads as "the floor got a bit lumpy." The impossibility lives in the geodesic, and geodesics are invisible on a 6-inch screen.

**A2 — What You Watch Backs Away (16). DEAD.** This idea deliberately rebuilds the exact bug that has already cost this project weeks. Read HANDOFF §18: "movement still appeared nonfunctional." A2's design goal is a player pressing the d-pad and not arriving. The escape — close your eyes and walk — is not discoverable by touch by anyone, ever; it is discoverable by frustration or by a sentence of text, and the owner has banned the sentence. A grid subtly spreading apart on a phone in daylight is not a legible cause either.

**A3 — The Doubling Door (30).** The best idea in Lens A by a distance, because it needs zero new controls and its rule is delivered by walking through a door — the most universally understood action in games. The grain of the floor becoming the walls is a genuinely visible impossible moment at phone size. The failure case is real, though, and the author names it: without the crack-that-becomes-a-doorway landing by loop two it is a treadmill with a size gimmick, and a corridor is the least interesting room this game could build.

**A4 — Left Comes Back Right (16). DEAD.** Inverting the d-pad on a phone does not read as revelation, it reads as "the game is broken and I am going to close the tab." This project's entire history is a fight to convince a player the controls work at all; A4's centrepiece is convincing them they don't. Worse, the current code *already* rotates screen-relative `fwd`/`rgt` 90° across the dimension shift, so an orientation flip lands on top of an existing axis swap and becomes indistinguishable from a camera bug. The Klein-bottle holonomy is a gift to the math major and nothing to anyone else.

**A5 — The Room in the Stone (25).** "Your six steps became a forty-metre mountain range" is a great image and it is straight off the handoff's wishlist. But cause and effect are separated by a scene transition, which is precisely how you lose a phone player, and the first thirty seconds are "tap a pebble, arrive in a room that looks the same." Nothing is fun yet at the thirty-second mark; you are still setting up. L build for a payoff you cannot feel until minute two.

**B1 — Lower the Lamp (31).** The strongest pure toy in the meta lens. Everybody on earth already knows what a lamp does to a shadow, so the rule is pre-installed — you drag, the black shape stretches, and it stretches *across the hole you can't cross*. Continuous, analog, immediately repeatable, and the "hold the eye and arrive where the shadow was" beat is a physical consequence of a thing you were already playing with. Two real problems: it only works once the game is in 3D with a real ground plane, and the swap must dolly under the veil or the player is lost in the dark.

**B2 — The Second Shadow (25).** The reveal is the handoff's own ultimate reveal and it is beautifully staged. But for the first thirty seconds it is a smudge on the floor that does nothing when you walk onto it — the author says so — and the discovery that you can *drag* it is a lucky accident, not a designed affordance. Then the reward for the discovery is a free-orbiting camera on a 6-inch screen, which is the thing the author correctly flags as panic-inducing. Great third act, no first act.

**B3 — Built Behind Your Back (16). DEAD.** The verb is "hold a button and look at a black screen while sounds happen." That is a demo you listen to, not a thing you do. It fails the harder test too: act two requires the player to spontaneously understand that "unobserved" means *outside the camera frustum* rather than *eyes closed* — that cannot be reached by touch, only by narration, which is banned. And it makes the eye button mean two contradictory things in the same game, poisoning the mechanic that already works in the slit room.

**B4 — One Right Place (16). DEAD.** Hunting for a single point in R³ using four arrow buttons on a phone. The author's line "the continuous convergence feedback is not optional" is a confession that without a hot-and-cold meter the puzzle is unplayable — and with one, it is a hot-and-cold meter. Anamorphosis is also the most decorative idea here: for ninety percent of its runtime it is a picture that becomes a nicer picture. And the payoff reveal ("the ring marks where your *eye* goes") requires a camera the game does not have and a paragraph the owner will not allow.

**B5 — Blink and Keep It (32).** Tap the eye, and a photograph of what you were just looking at tips over and lands on the floor at your feet. That is a three-second toy with no words and it is delightful before it means anything. Then you fold it with the exact drag your thumb already learned, and the far wall of the real room swings down — the composition of the game's two existing verbs into a third, with no new controls. The honest risk is the whole idea: if the real room doesn't move *hugely* and in the same frame, it's a UI gimmick. Build the scoped one-room scripted version; do not generalise first.

**C1 — Knock (27).** Press and release on the ground and things answer from the dark — an S-sized build, no new UI, and the knock-while-dragging-the-seam moment ("the far side is arriving sooner") states the game's entire thesis in one two-thumbed gesture with zero language. That is the best thing in Lens C. It is held back by being tap-to-reveal-the-map underneath, and by the author's own mitigation, which makes hearing the *only* way to find things — so a player on a muted phone in a noisy train is not playing a degraded game, they are playing no game.

**C2 — Shut (25).** The hole-in-the-hiss is a real perceptual mechanism that genuinely needs no training, and walking through a wall is a first-rate payoff. But the loop is again "hold a button, screen goes black, walk blind on a phone," and the author's fallback — render the audio as a brightness gradient on the veil — quietly admits the sound won't carry, at which point the mechanic is "walk toward the dark part." M–L build for a collision-set swap whose ugly case needed a whole invented sub-mechanic (the eye sticking shut) to paper over.

**C3 — Where the Wobble Stops (25).** Beats do survive a bad phone speaker, that engineering claim is true, and three pillars visibly welding into one object is a strong, cheap, visible impossible moment. But this is the interference room again wearing a different hat, and I'm the critic the author was predicting. Fatal detail: no visual redundancy at all. A silenced iPhone — the default state of most iPhones — turns this into a player wandering an empty floor with no feedback of any kind. C4 and E2 both solved this; C3 didn't.

**C4 — Tune (32).** The best single gesture in the twenty-five. One vertical thumb drag, the whole world's colour rotates, and a pillar you were walking through *thuds* into solidity — cause, effect and rule delivered in under two seconds with no words, and it is fun to scrub back and forth for its own sake. Crossing a gap on a bridge that only exists in the middle of the turn is a real impossible moment at phone size. It is also the only idea whose gesture is deliberately orthogonal to the existing fold drag, so the two compose in one diagonal swipe. Non-negotiable: pitch and luminance must carry the information as hard as hue does, and every hue setting must have something worth walking to.

**C5 — Eight Steps (16). DEAD.** A ±90 ms timing window, in Safari, over Bluetooth, in a project whose documented history is failing to make a *single tap* produce visible movement. This is malpractice on this input stack. And the author's own rescue kills it: if the tile always eventually solidifies and sloppiness only detunes the note, then tapping on the beat has no mechanical consequence and the mechanic is decoration. Nothing impossible is visible on screen either — a Z/8 action on a corridor is a math-major pleasure with no picture attached.

**D1 — The Second Point (27).** The last fifteen seconds are extraordinary: the camera pitches up, the line unrolls into the sheet, and the breathing wall is revealed as a rotating rectangle you were seeing edge-on. That is the handoff's cross-section thesis delivered wordlessly, cheaply, and it retro-teaches the endgame. It also fixes a real problem — it gives the player a tap that visibly causes something in a world simple enough that the cause is unmissable. But you cannot escape that fifty of those seconds are a dot on a line, and the author's hard cap on the runtime is an admission that nobody would want more. Nobody replays 1D.

**D2 — The Corner Comes to You (31).** The best extension of the mechanic this game has already chosen. Same grab, same drag, same shader, one perpendicular hinge — and the farthest light in the world lands a metre from your face with a mirrored copy of your body standing in it. Order-matters is the rare hidden-math beat that a child states correctly in their own words ("I did it in the wrong order") while a mathematician sees SO(3) non-commutativity. The real danger is graphical soup: a doubly folded plane on a small screen. The mirrored body is the right thing to anchor the eye on, and the ground under the folded quarter must dim, hard.

**D3 — Turning the Door (27).** Genuinely legible in three seconds — spin a frame, see somewhere else — and the 45° detent that nobody pointed at is a lovely, discoverable secret. But it is an L build that adds an entire second render path (portal targets, mirrored virtual cameras, blended-room collision) to a phone game that is currently one shader and a canvas texture, and it composes with almost nothing already written. The author's stated risk is the one that will happen: four channels on a television.

**D4 — The Flat Places (23).** The buttons sliding off the d-pad and dying with a descending tone is the most theatrical single beat in the set, and it costs nearly nothing because `dim` already drives every visual. But the first contact is the game taking your controls away, and the owner's brief is "more fun." A player who loses input on a phone assumes lag, not design — that is the exact ambiguity this project has been drowning in for weeks. And E3 does the same physics as a *toy you choose* an hour earlier, which makes D4 strictly the worse half of a good pair. Ship E3 and D4 becomes free later.

**D5 — The Rising Cut (27).** The purest expression of the handoff's final wish, and the one-day version (object, draggable plane, four snap heights, a ghost of your own recorded path inside each silhouette) is genuinely smart scoping. But its entire value is recognition, and recognition requires having already lived in the levels — so its score in the first thirty seconds of a new player's life is zero by construction. In the hand it is a vertical slider and an outline that changes. This is an ending, not a mechanic, and it should not be built until there is a game to end.

**E1 — Corners (29).** Flicking your own body into a spin with inertia is a real toy that a person does for no reason, and "every corner gives me a new way to turn" is the cleanest statement of perception-not-power in the whole set — you gain a rotation, never a weapon. The composition is excellent too: a flat three-corner slides under the fold crease, a four-corner gets folded *by* the seam instead. The risk is exactly as stated: a small spinning object on a 6-inch screen is noise unless each corner is individually identifiable by pitch and size, and the five-corner shadow is a scripted two seconds pretending to be a system.

**E2 — Octave (31).** Nearly free — the 22 landmark pillars are already in the code — and brushing past a reed that bends and rings is fun on contact with no instruction whatsoever. "Same note = same place" is one of the two or three most child-legible rules here, and walking into one pillar and out of another twenty units away, mid-stride, is a big visible impossibility. Crucially it does what C3 refused to do and makes pitch redundant with hue and with the speed of the band running up the pillar, so a muted phone still plays. The lockstep animation of a glued pair is not optional; get it wrong and the teleport is a bug.

**E3 — Thin (35).** "The player will walk in and out five times in a row. It feels like a trampoline." That is the only claim in twenty-five ideas I believe without argument, and it is the whole job. Walk into light, squash flat with a 0.3s overshoot, walk out, pop. No words, no button, no explanation, no risk of being read as a bug — and it is `dim` run backwards and locally, so the machinery already exists. Then it becomes a tool (a disc fits a 0.15 slot), then a perception shift (a gap with no width isn't there), then a rug-pull (the second column deletes a different axis and two rooms you visited separately were always one). Toy → rule → tool → revelation, in that order, on a control scheme with zero additions. This is the shape every other idea here was trying to be.

**E4 — Two Turns (17).** The Dirac belt trick is famously, notoriously impossible to see — the author writes this down and then proposes building a game beat on it anyway. On a phone, the second turn untangling the thread will be filed under "some glitchy thing happened" by every player alive. The mirrored world compounds it with inverted d-pad directions, which is the A4 failure again. High build cost (parallel-transport frames on a spline ribbon) for a payoff whose legibility the author cannot defend.

**E5 — Beads (30).** Tilt is the one input a phone has that nothing else does, and three hundred beads sloshing with a granular hiss is fun before the player has understood a single thing — which is exactly the owner's brief. It also does real work: pouring beads across a floor that looks level and watching them pool is surveying an invisible field with your hands, and beads pouring *across* the fold before the player knows the fold does that is a beautiful piece of foreshadowing. Two real hazards: the iOS DeviceOrientation permission dance will silently kill this for a chunk of players if the drag fallback is second-class, and if the first pool doesn't open something within fifteen seconds it is a screensaver.

## TOP 5

1. **E3 — Thin.** The only idea whose core is an unarguable toy: you squash, you pop, you do it again for no reason — and it costs almost nothing because `dim` already drives every visual in the game.
2. **C4 — Tune.** One thumb drag turns the whole world's colour and thuds a ghost pillar into solidity: cause, rule and consequence in under two seconds, on a gesture deliberately orthogonal to the fold.
3. **B5 — Blink and Keep It.** A photograph of your own view falling onto the floor is a toy on contact, and folding it with the drag the player already owns is the game's two verbs composing into a third with no new controls.
4. **B1 — Lower the Lamp.** Everybody already knows what a lamp does to a shadow, so the rule ships pre-installed; drag it down and the black shape reaches across the hole you couldn't cross.
5. **E2 — Octave.** Nearly free on the 22 pillars already in the scene, fun on first contact, and "same note = same place" is a rule a child says out loud before they've been told anything.

*(Honourable mention, 31: **D2 — The Corner Comes to You**, the best extension of the fold the game already has. It lost the fifth slot only on hand-feel: pulling two seams is smart, not fun.)*

## KILL LIST

- **A2 — What You Watch Backs Away.** Its first thirty seconds are the exact "movement appears nonfunctional" bug that has already cost this project weeks, rebuilt on purpose.
- **A4 — Left Comes Back Right.** An inverted d-pad on a phone reads as a broken build, and the current code already swaps the movement axes across the dimension shift, so the flip is indistinguishable from a camera bug.
- **B3 — Built Behind Your Back.** The verb is "hold a button and stare at black," and its real revelation cannot be reached by touch — only by a paragraph explaining that "unobserved" means outside the frustum.
- **B4 — One Right Place.** Hunting a single point in R³ with four arrow buttons; without a hot-and-cold meter it is unplayable, and with one it *is* a hot-and-cold meter.
- **C5 — Eight Steps.** Millisecond timing windows over Safari and Bluetooth on the one input stack this project has proven it cannot yet trust, and the author's own fix removes the mechanic entirely.
- **E4 — Two Turns.** Built on the one topological demonstration that is famous for being invisible, plus inverted controls on top.

## ONE THING I'D BUILD FIRST

**E3 — Thin.** It is the only idea where I can already picture the thumb: walk into the light, go flat with a springy overshoot, walk out, pop, do it again. No new button, no narration, no chance of reading as a bug, and `dim` already drives every visual so it is days not weeks. It also converts the game's planned punishment (dimensional collapse) into a toy the player chose first — which is what will make it terrifying later.
